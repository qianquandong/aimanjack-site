How to choose AI training for office managers in Dallas

The office manager is usually the person asked to look into AI for everybody else. This is the page to work through before booking anything.

Jack Qian · Published September 28, 2026

Key takeaways
  • AI training for office managers works when the office manager brings the work the office already repeats every week, not a shortlist of tools to evaluate.
  • Fill in a one-page brief before booking: who is in the room, what each role repeats weekly, which AI tools are approved, and which information must never go into them.
  • If nobody can name the approved tools or the information that is off limits, settle that first. Training on a tool the company has not approved teaches the wrong habit.
  • Three formats do three different jobs: 90 minutes to show a department one live build, a half day so everyone leaves with a workflow that runs, multi-week when workflows keep getting built and then dropped.
  • Two weeks after the session, ask whether each workflow was tested on real work, whether the output was accurate enough to use, and whether the task should continue, change or stop.

The one page that lands on the office manager

Section three of the free Team AI Training Brief asks for one line per role: the work that person repeats every week. The template's instructions say the manager is the one who fills it in, not for them, and that section 3 is the part only they know. That is also why AI training for office managers is a different job from training any one department: the tasks come from every desk in the place.

That is also the person who gets handed the question. An owner or a partner reads something about AI over the weekend, turns to whoever runs the office, and asks them to look into it. So tools get researched, two or three demos get booked, and nothing about Monday changes.

Why the office manager decides whether the training works

Our AI training is built for teams of roughly 5 to 60 people where most people do not write code: admin, front office, operations, sales, planning, customer service, and the managers who lead them. The office manager sits across all of it. They know which report gets rebuilt from the same spreadsheet every Monday, which email gets answered the same way forty times a month, and which folder holds the current price list.

They also hold the piece nobody else tracks: which information is sensitive. That one fact decides whether a task can be practised safely in a room full of people or not.

The rest of this post is what to prepare, how to choose between the three formats, and what to ask two weeks later.

How to plan AI training for office managers

  1. Write one line of repeating work per role. Not the hardest thing each person does — the most repetitive. Intake forms retyped into the system, the weekly report, appointment confirmations, vendor follow-ups, the same three paragraphs sent to every new client.
  2. Score each task on four questions. Does it happen often enough to matter? Can the input and the output be described clearly? Can a knowledgeable person check the result quickly? Can it be tested without exposing sensitive information? A task that fails any one of the four is not a first task.
  3. Settle tools and data boundaries before you book. Two lines: which AI tools the company has approved, and which information must not go into them. The AI tool approval checklist is there to get those two lines written. If either stays blank, fix that before a session, because a team trained on an unapproved tool learns a habit you will have to undo.
  4. Find out where the team actually is. The AI readiness assessment is ten multiple-choice questions across eight areas: people, usage, processes, knowledge, tools, governance, leadership and measurement. It takes about three minutes, needs no sign-up, and scores in your browser. The weak rows tell you what a first session has to cover.
  5. Pick the format for what you need to happen afterwards. Not for what fits the calendar. The table below is the honest version of that choice.
  6. Name the person who signs off. For each workflow on your list, write down who checks the output before it goes to a client, a vendor or a report. If there is no name, the workflow is not ready to run, whatever the training covered.
  7. Put the two-week review in the calendar now. Book it the same day you book the session, while everyone still thinks it is a good idea. The two-week workflow review is the three questions to ask.

When AI training for office managers does not work

It does not work when the office does not repeat anything. Some offices are genuinely one-off: every project has a different shape, every client is handled differently on purpose. Those teams score low on the frequency question for every candidate task, and a workshop turns back into a product discussion. A short briefing is more honest for them than a half day.

It does not work when the approved source does not exist. If the price list, the policy and the current procedure live in three peoples heads, there is nothing to point the AI at. That is a documentation problem, and no amount of training solves it. Write the source down first; it is usually a smaller job than it feels.

It also does not work when the office manager has no authority to change how work gets done. If every change needs a partner meeting, the session ends with good intentions and a calendar that looks the same. Get the decision-maker into the room, even for the last thirty minutes.

And if the real problem is that the phone rings while everyone is with a customer, training the team is the wrong fix. That is a staffing gap during working hours, and an AI receptionist answers it directly. Training teaches people to use AI on work they already do; it does not add a person to the front desk.

What to do in the next 14 days

  • Fill in the team brief for your own office: roles in the room, one repeating task per role, approved tools, off-limits information. About twenty minutes.
  • Run the ten-question readiness assessment and write down your two weakest rows. Three minutes.
  • Take the highest-scoring task on your list and ask the person who does it today how they would check an AI draft of it. If they cannot answer in a sentence, pick the next task instead.

Questions people ask

What should an office manager bring to AI training?

One line of repeating work per role in the office, the list of AI tools the company has approved, and the list of information that must not go into them. Everything in a session is built on top of those three lists, and the office manager is usually the only person who can produce all of them.

Do office managers need any technical background for AI training?

No. The sessions are designed for teams where most people do not write code, including admin and front office roles. The instruction template is written in plain language and the verification checklist fits on one page.

How long should AI training for an office team take?

A 90-minute session suits a department that needs a first look. A half-day hands-on workshop suits a team that must leave with a workflow that runs, and takes up to ten people at your office. A multi-week program suits a team where workflows keep getting started and dropped.

Can AI training be run at our office in Dallas?

Yes. Sessions run in person anywhere in the Dallas-Fort Worth metroplex, including Dallas, Fort Worth, Plano, Richardson, Frisco, McKinney and Arlington, and remotely over video for teams outside DFW or split across offices. They run in English or Chinese.

How do we know whether the AI training worked?

Two weeks after the session, check three things on each workflow: was it tested on real work, was the result accurate enough to use, and should the task continue, change or stop. Attendance and satisfaction scores do not tell you any of that.
