Pre-training checklist

An AI data-safety checklist for workplace training

Before employees practice with company information, define the approved tools, prohibited data, access boundaries, verification steps, and the person responsible when something goes wrong.

By Jack · Published July 17, 2026 · 6 minute read

Before an AI workshop: name the approved tools, prohibit sensitive data, use sanitized practice material, limit connected accounts, require human approval for consequential actions, and give participants a clear way to report mistakes. Company policy and legal requirements always take priority over a general checklist.

Why safety belongs inside the lesson

A warning at the end of a workshop is easy to forget. Safety works better when every exercise includes the same questions: Is this information approved for the tool? Can the output be checked? Does a person approve what happens next?

This checklist is educational, not legal, security, or compliance advice. Organizations in regulated industries should involve their security, privacy, legal, and compliance teams before adopting a tool.

1. Approved tools and accounts

  • List the AI products employees may use for work.
  • Specify whether personal accounts are prohibited.
  • Confirm which paid or enterprise settings apply to company data.
  • Document who can approve a new tool or integration.
  • Use sanitized examples if policy is not yet settled.

2. Prohibited information

Write a short, visible list of information that must not enter an unapproved AI tool. Depending on the organization, that may include:

  • Passwords, API keys, access tokens, or security details
  • Customer personal information
  • Employee records and health information
  • Confidential contracts, financial data, or unreleased plans
  • Source code, designs, or other protected intellectual property
  • Information covered by a client agreement or regulation

“Do not paste confidential data” is too vague unless employees know what the organization considers confidential.

3. Safe practice material

  • Create fictional or anonymized examples for workshop exercises.
  • Remove names, account numbers, addresses, and internal identifiers.
  • Use a small excerpt when the complete document is unnecessary.
  • Check that screenshots do not reveal browser tabs, notifications, or customer data.
  • Do not assume deleting a chat reverses every data-handling consequence.

4. Connected tools and permissions

Agents and integrations deserve special care because they can act, not just answer. Before connecting email, cloud storage, calendars, customer systems, or publishing tools:

  • Grant the narrowest access the workflow needs.
  • Prefer read-only access during early tests.
  • Use a test environment or limited folder when possible.
  • Require approval before sending, deleting, purchasing, publishing, or changing a system of record.
  • Record which person owns the integration and can disable it.

5. Output verification

  • Trace important claims back to an approved source.
  • Review calculations, names, dates, quotes, and policy statements.
  • Do not treat confident wording as evidence.
  • Define what requires subject-matter review.
  • Keep a human responsible for consequential decisions.

6. Mistakes and incident response

Employees should know what to do if restricted information is entered or an agent takes the wrong action. Provide a named contact and a simple reporting path. The first response should focus on containment and facts, not blame; people hide mistakes when reporting feels dangerous.

Record the tool, account, information involved, time, action taken, and who was notified. Follow the organization's existing security or privacy response process.
