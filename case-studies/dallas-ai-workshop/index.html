First-party workshop case study

Inside a hands-on Dallas AI workshop for 30+ professionals

A practical AI class should help people connect a tool to the work already on their desk. This is a documented look at how Jack taught that approach to a full room of working professionals in Dallas.

By Jack · Published July 17, 2026 · Dallas, Texas

What happened: Jack led a live, hands-on AI workshop in Dallas for a room of more than 30 working professionals. The session focused on using AI for recognizable business tasks, choosing the right level of tool, writing useful instructions, checking outputs, and protecting sensitive information.

The workshop goal

The purpose was not to turn participants into AI engineers. It was to make AI less abstract and give working professionals a practical way to judge where it could help. That starts with the task, not the tool.

A useful workshop question is not “What can AI do?” The better question is “Which repeated part of this job takes time, uses information, and produces an output a person can check?” That framing gives participants a safer place to begin.

How the session was structured

  1. Start with familiar work. Examples came from email, documents, spreadsheets, research, and preparation tasks rather than technical demonstrations.
  2. Explain the tool choice. Participants learned the difference between asking a chatbot for an answer and using an agent or workflow to move through several steps.
  3. Make instructions testable. A useful prompt identifies the goal, source material, constraints, output format, and the checks a person should make.
  4. Show the failure points. The class included confident but incorrect answers, missing context, privacy risks, and the need for human approval.
  5. Connect the lesson to tomorrow's work. Participants were encouraged to choose one small, repeatable task instead of attempting a broad “AI transformation.”

What the workshop covered

  • Drafting and improving work email without giving up final review
  • Summarizing long documents and checking the summary against the source
  • Cleaning and organizing spreadsheet information
  • Researching a topic or prospect before a meeting
  • Recognizing when a chatbot is enough and when an agent may be useful
  • Keeping customer, employee, and company information out of unapproved tools

The examples were deliberately ordinary. That is where most teams find their first useful AI workflow: not in a dramatic demo, but in a repeated task that can be described clearly and checked quickly.

What this case study can verify

This page documents a real classroom, a real instructor, and a room of more than 30 participants through first-party photographs. It does not claim a named client, a specific venue, participant testimonials, or a measured productivity result because those details are not published here.

That distinction matters. Trustworthy AI education should be clear about the evidence it has and equally clear about what it has not measured.

What Jack carried into future training

Large-group training works best when each concept returns to a recognizable task. Participants do not need a catalog of products. They need a simple framework for choosing a task, supplying context, checking the answer, and deciding whether the workflow is safe enough to repeat.

That same structure now anchors AI Man Jack's hands-on AI training for Dallas professionals and teams. Teams can also use the non-technical AI training curriculum to prepare before a session.
