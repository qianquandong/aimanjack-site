Dallas-area AI hackathon: teams built and demoed working products

On April 18, 2026, Jack Qian organized a five-and-a-half-hour AI hackathon in Fairview, Texas. The Luma record lists 37 guests, and every team was required to demonstrate a working prototype or running code.

What happened: Participants selected one of three roles, formed three-person teams, and spent three focused hours building an AI product. Teams then delivered five-minute live demonstrations. A real prototype or running code was required; slide-only presentations did not qualify.

A format designed to move from ideas to execution

The event began by giving each participant a role. The “idea” role focused on the problem, logic, and product definition. The “code” role handled development and technical implementation. The “visual” role focused on design, presentation, and communicating the product clearly.

Participants used their role markers to find complementary teammates. Once three people formed a team, they registered the group and moved into the build area.

The build-day schedule

  1. 1:00-1:20 PM. Rules and roles. Participants received role markers and the challenge requirements.
  2. 1:20-2:00 PM. Team formation. Attendees compared ideas and skills, then formed three-person teams.
  3. 2:00-5:00 PM. Build sprint. Teams had three hours to make the core function work and prepare a demonstration.
  4. 5:00-5:45 PM. Live demos. Each team had five minutes to show a prototype or running code.
  5. 5:45-6:00 PM. Judging and awards. The event concluded with scoring, recognition, and a group photograph.

The page does not claim a measured business outcome, a named sponsor, or a specific number of completed products because those facts are not documented in the event record available for publication.

Why a build sprint teaches something a lecture cannot

A lecture can explain what an AI workflow looks like. A hackathon exposes the harder questions: Can the team define one problem clearly? Can they divide responsibilities? Can they make the main function work under time pressure? Can they show the result to another person?

That build-first approach now informs Jack's hands-on AI training for Dallas teams. The goal is to leave with an observable workflow, not just a list of tools.
